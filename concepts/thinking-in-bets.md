# Thinking in Bets — separating decision quality from outcome quality

A mental model for judging decisions made under uncertainty, drawn from Annie Duke's book
*Thinking in Bets* and extended with a practical scoring framework.

**Primary source:** [If You Only Read One Book This Year, Make It This](https://youtu.be/nc4u9oxb0nA)
— Sandeep Swadia, 18:21, published 2026-08-06.
[Source: YouTube, https://youtu.be/nc4u9oxb0nA, transcript retrieved 2026-08-09]

---

## Core idea: resulting

The central error is **judging the quality of a decision by the quality of its outcome**.
The two are only loosely connected. Annie Duke's background is poker — a domain where you
can play a hand correctly and lose, or play it terribly and win.
[Source: YouTube, https://youtu.be/nc4u9oxb0nA, 2026-08-09]

> "Good results are bad teachers. The most dangerous teacher in your life is accidental
> success."

The illustrative case: a drunk driver who runs three red lights at 2am and gets home safely
had a *good outcome* from a *terrible decision* — and will now be more likely to repeat it.
[Source: YouTube, https://youtu.be/nc4u9oxb0nA, 2026-08-09]

### Self-serving bias makes this worse

The asymmetry is predictable and applies to everyone:

| | We win | We lose |
|---|---|---|
| **Us** | our skill | bad luck |
| **Others** | their luck | their lack of skill |

[Source: YouTube, https://youtu.be/nc4u9oxb0nA, 2026-08-09]

---

## The skill/luck audit

Rather than asking "was I skilled or lucky?" — a question self-serving bias has already
rigged — imagine *someone else* making the identical move, then place them on a dial from
pure luck to pure skill (90/10, 50/50). That number is more honest.
[Source: YouTube, https://youtu.be/nc4u9oxb0nA, 2026-08-09]

Swadia's worked example of his own career decision, decomposed:

| Element | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Choosing the CSO role at the larger public company over a CEO role at a 150-person startup | **Skill** — suppressed ego, ran the framework |
| That company's stock rising 500% | **Luck** — industry timing |
| Failing to sell after retiring, before a 90% collapse | **Neither** — a plain mistake, driven by greed and emotional attachment |

[Source: YouTube, https://youtu.be/nc4u9oxb0nA, 2026-08-09]

The point of the audit is not self-flagellation over luck you never controlled. It is to
**audit the process, extract the lesson, and know when to fold.**

## Certainty is not available — stop waiting for it

Every decision is a bet against the future; you are choosing between multiple possible
futures with incomplete information. If you knew everything, it wouldn't be a bet.
[Source: YouTube, https://youtu.be/nc4u9oxb0nA, 2026-08-09]

**More information does not reliably help.** Sheena Iyengar's Columbia supermarket jam
study: a 24-jam tasting table drew more visitors than a 6-jam table, but the 6-jam group
*bought more*. More options produced analysis paralysis, not confidence.
[Source: YouTube, https://youtu.be/nc4u9oxb0nA, 2026-08-09]

**Chess vs poker.** Chess is hard but fully observable — every piece is visible. Poker
hides the other players' cards and the next card. Life is poker, not chess: you never see
the full board.

> **Practical move — the two-column table.** Column 1: known facts, what is actually
> visible. Column 2: hidden cards — what is unknown, what am I assuming, what am I *hoping*
> is true. Separating the two makes the decision cleaner.

## Beliefs distort what you see

The *They Saw a Game* study: Dartmouth and Princeton students watched the same football
game between their universities. Dartmouth fans saw Princeton play dirty; Princeton fans
saw the exact opposite. Same footage, same penalties.
[Source: YouTube, https://youtu.be/nc4u9oxb0nA, 2026-08-09]

This is why misinformation is sticky — it feeds what we already want to believe. The
"red wine is good for your heart" claim persists despite repeated debunking, because people
enjoy drinking red wine.

**Two counter-moves:**

1. **Replace binary thinking with probabilities.** Not "right or wrong" but "how confident
   am I — 70%? 80%?"
2. **"Wanna bet?"** When you feel certain, imagine putting $100 or $1,000 on the belief. It
   forces you to state exactly what you believe and on what evidence.

## AVA / EVA — Expected Value Analysis

Swadia's own four-step framework, going beyond the book. Emotions get a seat at the table,
but AVA gets the first vote.
[Source: YouTube, https://youtu.be/nc4u9oxb0nA, 2026-08-09]

1. **Map the scenarios.** Four or five branches on paper: great success, moderate success,
   minor failure, catastrophic failure.
2. **Assign payoffs.** A figure per branch — and not only cash. Time, reputation, health,
   impact on family, whatever actually matters to you.
3. **Assign odds.** A probability per branch, based on what you know *today*.
4. **Calculate expected value.** Payoff × probability.

> The evaluation process matters more than the precision of the numbers. The real value is
> the stress test: imagine it is 12 months from now and the thing has failed completely,
> then write the specific story of how it failed. **Scripting the disaster before it happens
> builds the exit ramps before the crash.**

AVA doesn't make a decision certain. It clarifies *why* it is uncertain.

## Tilt and regret

**Tilt** is the poker term for the moment the last hand infects the next one — you play
correctly, lose anyway, and anger or wounded pride takes over. The inverse is equally
dangerous: win big, start betting like you're invincible. Both are illusions.

The trap: **when you are tilting it is obvious to everyone except you.**
[Source: YouTube, https://youtu.be/nc4u9oxb0nA, 2026-08-09]

Regret is the mechanism that keeps the last hand alive inside the next one. It should heal,
not harm. Losing money shouldn't make you exit just as the market recovers; being fired
shouldn't stop you applying for the next role.

> "The rear view mirror can't steer the car." — hang your regrets where you can see them,
> then drive forward. A regret is only wasted if it doesn't improve the next bet.

---

## Applying this

- Run a **skill/luck audit** on outcomes before drawing lessons from them — especially wins.
- Use the **two-column table** to separate known from assumed before committing.
- State confidence as a **percentage**, not a binary.
- Run **AVA** on decisions with asymmetric downside, and always script the failure case.
- Watch for **tilt** after both losses *and* outsized wins.
