# The 7 Types of Meetings and Their Emotional Terrain

## Understanding Emotional Terrain

**Emotional terrain** = the underlying emotional dynamics that drive behavior in the meeting.

Understanding the terrain tells you:
- What will trigger people
- How people will react
- What they're actually protecting (not just what they're arguing about)
- How to position yourself for advantage

Musashi studied terrain before battles. Meetings are the same.

---

## Type 1: Status Update Meeting

### What It Appears To Be
"Let's sync on where we are with Project X. What's the update?"

### The Emotional Terrain

**Apparent stakes:** Low. It's just information sharing.

**Real stakes:** 
- Each person is being *evaluated* ("Are you on track? Competent? Worth keeping?")
- Each person is *defending their work* ("Did I mess up? Will I be blamed?")
- Each person is *competing for attention/resources* ("Will my work be recognized?")

**Common emotional currents:**
- **Anxiety:** "Will I be judged?"
- **Frustration:** "Why do I have to explain this again?"
- **Shame:** "I haven't finished what I said I would"
- **Irritation:** "This is a waste of my time"
- **One-upmanship:** "My update is more important than theirs"

### Typical Reactions

- Some people over-detail (protecting themselves with information)
- Some people minimize problems (hoping no one notices)
- Some people interrupt others' updates (asserting dominance)
- Some people check out (protecting themselves with disengagement)
- Some people ask hard questions (testing credibility)

### Your Emotional Triggers Here

- Being questioned about your progress (feels like judgment)
- Someone minimizing your work (feels disrespected)
- Being interrupted (feels powerless)
- Silence/lack of recognition (feels invisible)
- Someone else getting more air time (feels competitive)

### Preparation Strategy

1. **Before the meeting:**
   - Know your narrative (what you did, why, what's next, what you need)
   - Anticipate questions ("Why is this behind schedule?" "What's the risk?")
   - Have numbers/evidence ready (removes defensiveness)
   - Know one thing you're proud of (sets tone of competence)

2. **Your goal (pick one):**
   - Information clarity (everyone leaves knowing the status)
   - Visibility (your work is seen and appreciated)
   - Problem-solving (blockers are addressed)
   - Team cohesion (we're moving together)

3. **Emotional trigger preparation:**
   - "If I'm questioned sharply, I'll treat it as genuine curiosity, not attack"
   - "If I'm interrupted, I'll calmly finish my thought"
   - "If someone else gets more attention, it's not a referendum on my work"

### Execution Strategy

**Your opening:**
> "Here's where we are: on track for Q4 delivery, one blocker with the API integration (we're working with the platform team), and I need a decision on scope by Friday."

**Why this works:**
- Status (clear): on track
- Problem (honest): blocker exists
- Action (moving): working on it
- What you need (specific): decision

This positions you as competent and solution-oriented.

**If questioned:**
- Answer fully, don't defend
- *"The API integration is taking longer because the platform team has it lower priority. We're escalating now, but it could impact the timeline."* (honest, not defensive)

**If challenged:**
- Separate fact from opinion
- *"That's fair feedback. Here's what I'm doing about it..."* (shows you're responsive)

### Success Looks Like

- People understand your status
- You're asked follow-up questions (shows interest, not attack)
- Your blockers are acknowledged and addressed
- You leave feeling seen and competent (not invisible or defensive)

## Type 2: Decision-Making Meeting

### What It Appears To Be
"We need to decide between Option A, B, or C. What do you think?"

### The Emotional Terrain

**Apparent stakes:** High. A decision will be made.

**Real stakes:** Way higher.
- Each person has already mentally committed to an option (ego attachment)
- Each person is protecting a preference (their initiative, their department, their idea)
- Each person is competing for resources/priority/influence
- The decision will change someone's workload or status

**Common emotional currents:**
- **Attachment:** "I already decided this is the right answer"
- **Fear:** "My preferred option will be rejected"
- **Defensiveness:** "I have to convince them I'm right"
- **Territoriality:** "This decision affects my domain"
- **Resentment (pre-emptive):** "If they choose that, I'm going to be frustrated"

### Typical Reactions

- **Committed lobbyists:** People who argue hard for their option
- **Silent blockers:** People who disagree but stay quiet (then sabotage later)
- **Fence-sitters:** People who go along with whoever is most confident
- **Data people:** People who use facts to hide emotional preference
- **Dominators:** People who declare the decision before discussion ends

### Your Emotional Triggers Here

- Your preferred option being attacked (feels like personal attack)
- Someone else getting support for their option (feels like you're losing)
- Being asked for data you don't have (feels like you're unprepared)
- The decision being made without full consideration (feels unheard)
- Your concerns being dismissed (feels disrespected)

### Preparation Strategy

1. **Know the decision landscape:**
   - What options are actually on the table?
   - Who prefers which option (know this before the meeting)
   - Why does each person prefer their option?
   - What unstated concerns exist?

2. **Build your case:**
   - Data (not emotional, factual)
   - Trade-offs (show you understand the costs, not just benefits)
   - Impact (who wins, who loses, why)
   - Implementation (make it real, not theoretical)

3. **Understand the real decision-maker:**
   - Who actually decides? (Not always the person running the meeting)
   - What do they care about? (Revenue? Risk? Speed? Team morale?)
   - What would move them?

4. **Your goal:**
   - Is it to win your option? Or to ensure the best option wins?
   - These are different. Knowing which one changes your strategy.

### Execution Strategy

**Your opening:**
> "I've been thinking about this. Each option has real trade-offs. Option A is fastest but riskiest. Option B is safer but slower. Option C is middle ground but requires resources we don't have. I'd rather we choose well than choose fast. Here's what I think matters most: [state your criteria]. What do you think?"

**Why this works:**
- You show you've thought deeply
- You acknowledge all options (not dismissing others' preferences)
- You name the actual trade-offs
- You invite genuine discussion (not just selling your view)

**If your option is attacked:**
- Don't defend it. Strengthen the criteria.
- *"That's a fair point about the risk. That's exactly why we need to be clear: are we optimizing for speed or safety?"* (moves to principle, not your option)

**If another option is gaining support:**
- Ask genuine questions (not rhetorical):
- *"Help me understand the appeal of that option. What am I missing?"*
- Listen. You might change your mind. That's okay.

**If decision is made that you disagreed with:**
- Support it publicly, note the concern privately
- *"I had concerns about this, but the team decided. Let me help make it work."*

### Success Looks Like

- A decision is made clearly
- The group understands the trade-offs
- Even people who disagreed respect the process
- You can support the decision (even if not your preference)
- The decision has a clear owner (not orphaned)

## Type 3: Conflict Resolution Meeting

### What It Appears To Be
"We need to talk about the tension between your team and theirs."

### The Emotional Terrain

**Apparent stakes:** We're fixing a problem.

**Real stakes:** People are protecting their identity.
- One person (or team) feels blamed
- One person feels wronged
- There's a history of grievance
- People are protecting their reputation
- Someone fears consequences (job security, respect, future opportunities)

**Common emotional currents:**
- **Shame:** "I'm being called out"
- **Anger:** "I was wronged and no one cares"
- **Fear:** "This could cost me my job / my team's standing"
- **Defensiveness:** "I have to prove I didn't do what they're claiming"
- **Self-righteousness:** "I'm right and they're wrong"
- **Resigned hopelessness:** "This will never change"

### Typical Reactions

- **Stonewalling:** Refusing to engage, saying "I'm fine"
- **Blame-shifting:** "They started it"
- **Over-explaining:** Long defensive narratives
- **Attacking back:** "Well, they did X, Y, Z"
- **Intellectual distance:** Using data/logic to avoid emotion
- **Shutdown:** Going silent, checking out

### Your Emotional Triggers Here

- Being blamed (activates shame/anger)
- Not being heard (activates frustration/powerlessness)
- The other person not taking responsibility (activates anger/injustice)
- Fear that you'll be the one who has to give in (activates resentment)
- Old hurts being brought up again (activates fatigue/hopelessness)

### Preparation Strategy

1. **Know your part:**
   - Not the entire conflict, but what *you* actually contributed
   - Don't minimize it, don't exaggerate it
   - Separate facts from your interpretation

2. **Understand their perspective:**
   - Why do they feel wronged?
   - What's their legitimate concern?
   - What do they think you did?
   - What would resolution look like to them?

3. **Know what you want:**
   - Is it to win the argument? (Not productive)
   - Is it to fix the relationship? (Productive)
   - Is it to be understood? (Usually the real need)
   - Is it to prevent this from happening again? (Practical)

4. **Your goal:**
   - *"My goal is to understand where they're coming from and find a way forward that works for both of us."*
   - NOT: *"My goal is to prove I was right."*

### Execution Strategy

**Your opening:**
> "I know there's been tension, and I hate it. I want to understand what happened from your perspective, and I want you to understand mine. I'm not here to convince you I'm right. I'm here because I care about working well with you."

**Why this works:**
- Vulnerability (disarms defensiveness)
- Curiosity (not blame)
- Clear intent (not hidden agenda)
- Respect for their view (even though you disagree)

**If they attack you:**
- Don't defend. Listen.
- *"I hear you. That's how you experienced it. Help me understand more."*
- Your job isn't to make them wrong. It's to be understood.

**When you speak about your part:**
- Own it fully
- *"You're right that I didn't follow up as promised. That was on me."* (not: "I was busy")
- Explain what led to it (context, not excuse)
- Say what you'll do differently

**The bridge:**
- Find something you agree on
- *"I think we both care about getting projects done well. I think we both felt like the other person wasn't listening. Is that fair?"*

### Success Looks Like

- Both people feel heard
- Both people acknowledge their part
- A specific agreement about going forward (not vague promises)
- Reduced defensiveness (not necessarily friendship)
- The ability to work together again

## Type 4: Pitch/Proposal Meeting

### What It Appears To Be
"Here's my idea. Let me tell you why it's good."

### The Emotional Terrain

**Apparent stakes:** I'm presenting an idea.

**Real stakes (for you):**
- Your competence is being judged
- Your credibility is on trial
- Rejection feels personal
- Approval validates you
- You have something at stake emotionally

**Real stakes (for them):**
- They're evaluating risk (your idea could fail)
- They're protecting their resources (time, money, reputation)
- They're protecting their standing (if your idea fails, does that reflect on them?)
- They're skeptical (your confidence might be naive)
- They're looking for reasons to say no (it's easier than yes)

**Common emotional currents (in them):**
- **Skepticism:** "I doubt this will work"
- **Protectiveness:** "I don't want to waste resources"
- **Fear of missing out:** "But what if it does work?"
- **Gatekeeping:** "I need to make sure you're not wasting my time"

**Common emotional currents (in you):**
- **Anxiety:** "They're going to think this is stupid"
- **Desperation:** "I really need them to say yes"
- **Over-confidence:** "This is obviously good, why don't they get it?"
- **Shame:** "They're not interested"
- **Frustration:** "They're not asking the right questions"

### Typical Reactions (In Them)

- **Harsh questions:** Testing your conviction or finding holes
- **Silence:** No enthusiasm, no rejection
- **Defer:** "Let me think about it / talk to the team"
- **Nitpick:** Focusing on small details instead of the big picture
- **Redirect:** "That's interesting, but here's what we really need"

### Your Emotional Triggers Here

- Questions that feel like attacks on your idea
- Lack of enthusiasm (feels like rejection)
- Not understanding what you're proposing (feels like you explained badly)
- Someone pointing out a flaw (feels like failure)
- Silence after you finish (feels like "no")

### Preparation Strategy

1. **Know your audience:**
   - What do they care about? (Revenue? Risk? Impact? Feasibility?)
   - What are they skeptical about?
   - What's their decision process?
   - What would make them say yes? What would make them say no?

2. **Separate your idea from yourself:**
   - Your idea can be questioned without you being wrong
   - Their skepticism is their job, not a referendum on you
   - Good ideas get challenged; bad ideas get dismissed

3. **Anticipate objections:**
   - Cost: *"What's the ROI?"* (have an answer)
   - Risk: *"What could go wrong?"* (have a mitigation plan)
   - Feasibility: *"Can we actually do this?"* (have a timeline)
   - Fit: *"Does this align with strategy?"* (know how to connect dots)

4. **Your goal:**
   - NOT: *"Get them to say yes to my idea"*
   - BETTER: *"Help them understand why this matters and whether it fits"*

### Execution Strategy

**Your opening:**
> "I've got something I want to explore with you. It came from [what triggered it]. The opportunity is [clear benefit]. I know it's not without risk [acknowledge]. I want to see if you think it's worth pursuing. Let me walk through it, and I want your real feedback."

**Why this works:**
- You show thinking (not just enthusiasm)
- You acknowledge risks (builds credibility)
- You invite genuine feedback (not just selling)

**When they question you:**
- Treat it as curiosity, not attack
- *"Great question. The cost is X, and we'd need to see Y result to justify it."*
- If you don't know the answer: *"That's a great point I hadn't fully thought through. Let me dig into that and get back to you."* (Shows thoughtfulness, not weakness)

**If they're not enthusiastic:**
- Ask: *"What's your gut reaction? What concerns you most?"*
- Listen to their real concern (usually different from the questions they ask)

**If they say no:**
- Find the reason (is it timing? Fit? Resources? Feasibility?)
- *"I hear you. What would need to change for this to be worth exploring again?"*
- This isn't defeat. This is data for your next iteration.

### Success Looks Like

- They understand what you're proposing (even if they don't approve)
- They respect your thinking (even if they disagree)
- You understand their objections (even if you think they're wrong)
- A clear next step (approval, more research, revisit in X weeks, or clear no)
- You learned something about their priorities

## Type 5: One-on-One Meeting (Feedback/Relationship)

### What It Appears To Be
"Let's sync. How are you doing?"

### The Emotional Terrain

**Apparent stakes:** Just a check-in.

**Real stakes:**
- If your manager: They're evaluating you ("Are you performing? Improving? Worth developing?")
- If giving feedback: They're being judged ("Am I failing? Is my job at risk?")
- If receiving feedback: You're being tested ("Can I give them honest feedback and have them receive it?")
- Trust is either being built or eroded
- The relationship is on trial

**Common emotional currents:**
- **Vulnerability:** "Will I be judged?"
- **Hiding:** "What can I safely share?"
- **Seeking validation:** "Do they think I'm good?"
- **Anxiety:** "Is this a setup for bad news?"
- **Guardedness:** "I need to be careful"

### Typical Reactions

- **Over-share:** Dumping problems, seeking sympathy
- **Minimize:** Everything's fine, don't show weakness
- **Defensive:** If feedback is given, immediately explaining why it's not their fault
- **Performative:** Playing the role they think the other person wants
- **Avoidant:** Staying surface-level, redirecting

### Your Emotional Triggers Here

- Being vulnerable and them judging
- Being criticized (if this is a feedback one-on-one)
- Them not remembering what mattered to you last time
- Not being asked real questions (feels like they don't care)
- Them sharing about their problems (feels self-centered)

### Preparation Strategy

1. **Know what you want from this meeting:**
   - To be heard?
   - To get feedback?
   - To discuss your growth?
   - To address a conflict?
   - To check alignment?

2. **If giving feedback:**
   - Be specific (not general)
   - Be actionable (not just criticism)
   - Have an example
   - Know what you want to happen

3. **If receiving feedback:**
   - Come open (not defensive)
   - Listen fully before responding
   - Don't explain/defend immediately

4. **Your goal:**
   - Mutual understanding, not agreement
   - Honesty, not performance

### Execution Strategy

**Your opening:**
> "I wanted to make space for us to really connect. How are you actually doing? And I want to give you some feedback that I think will help."

**If giving feedback:**
- Start with appreciation (context)
- *"Here's what I observed..."* (specific, not character judgment)
- *"I think it matters because..."* (why this feedback)
- *"What would help?"* (invite their solution)

**If receiving feedback:**
- Listen without defending
- *"I'm taking that in. Can you give me an example?"* (clarity, not challenge)
- *"What do you see me doing well on this front?"* (balance)

**If things get emotional:**
- Pause, don't proceed
- *"This clearly matters to you. Let's take a beat."*
- Return to your goal: understanding, not winning

### Success Looks Like

- Both people feel heard
- No defensiveness (or defensiveness is named and moved past)
- Specific understanding of what's working and what isn't
- A shared sense of what comes next
- Trust is strengthened (or repair has begun)

## Type 6: High-Stakes Meeting (Promotion, Firing, Restructuring)

### What It Appears To Be
"We need to talk about your role / your future / the team restructure."

### The Emotional Terrain

**Apparent stakes:** Life-changing.

**Real stakes (if you):**
- Your livelihood (job security)
- Your identity (who you are in your career)
- Your future (trajectory, opportunities)
- Your dignity (how you're treated)
- Your family (financial security)

**Real stakes (if they):**
- Fairness (they want to be just)
- Safety (they're making a hard decision)
- Consequences (backlash, team morale)
- Legal exposure (did they handle this right?)

**Common emotional currents:**
- **Dread:** "This is going to be bad"
- **Numb shock:** If unexpected
- **Anger:** If unfair
- **Desperation:** *"I need this job"*
- **Shame:** If fired, or passed over for promotion
- **Disbelief:** *"This can't be happening"*

### Typical Reactions

- **Shutdown:** Can't think, can't speak
- **Anger outburst:** Saying things you'll regret
- **Negotiation:** *"But I could..."*
- **Denial:** *"You must be mistaken"*
- **Blame:** *"That's not fair. Here's why..."*

### Your Emotional Triggers Here

- Being told something you didn't expect (if bad news)
- Feeling powerless (outcome is decided)
- Being judged (even if fairly)
- Not being asked your perspective (feels unheard)
- Not having time to process (disorienting)

### Preparation Strategy (Before You Know There's A Meeting)

1. **If you suspect this might be a firing/restructure:**
   - Know your rights (legal/HR)
   - Have a plan (what's next, financially)
   - Identify your support system (who can help)
   - Prepare your mind (best case, worst case, middle case)

2. **If you're hoping for a promotion:**
   - Don't over-hope (prepare for "not yet")
   - Know what you'd ask for if rejected
   - Know what you'll do if it doesn't happen

3. **Emotional prep:**
   - *"Whatever happens, I'll handle it."*
   - *"I don't need to win here. I need to understand and protect myself."*

### Execution Strategy

**Your opening (when it happens):**
- Listen fully before speaking
- Don't react immediately (you're in shock)
- Ask clarifying questions (not defensive questions)

**If bad news:**
- *"I need a moment to process this. Can you give me the specifics?"*
- Get everything in writing
- Don't negotiate on the spot
- *"Thank you for being clear. I need to consult with my [family/lawyer/advisor] before I respond."*

**If good news:**
- Celebrate cautiously
- Get the specifics in writing
- Clarify expectations

**If disappointing news (not promoted, etc.):**
- *"I'm disappointed. Can you help me understand the feedback?"*
- Ask what you can do to get there next time
- Don't defend or over-explain

### Success Looks Like

- You understand exactly what's happening
- You know next steps
- Everything is documented
- You're not emotional (you'll have that later, privately)
- You maintain dignity
- You protect your interests

## The Meta-Pattern Across All Meeting Types

### What Triggers Happen in Every Meeting

1. **Being unseen** ("They don't understand my perspective")
2. **Being judged** ("I'm being evaluated negatively")
3. **Losing control** ("The outcome is out of my hands")
4. **Not being heard** ("My voice doesn't matter")
5. **Ego threat** ("I look incompetent / wrong / weak")

### Your Emotional Distance Strategy for Every Meeting Type

1. **Before:** Prepare so well you remove surprises (removes panic)
2. **During:** Notice which trigger is activating (name it internally)
3. **Response:** Respond from your goal, not your trigger
4. **After:** Debrief and build credibility for next time

### The Universal Opening

Regardless of meeting type:
> "[State purpose clearly]. I know this matters [acknowledge stakes]. My goal is to [state your goal, not your position]. I want to hear your perspective too. Can we [specific action]?"

This works because it:
- Shows you've thought about it (competence)
- Acknowledges stakes (respect)
- States your intent (honesty)
- Invites their input (collaboration)

### Preparation Depth By Stakes

| Meeting Type | Prep Time | Prep Depth | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Status Update | 15 min | Light | Low stakes, but builds credibility |
| Decision-Making | 1-2 hours | Medium | High stakes, multiple agendas |
| Conflict Resolution | 2+ hours | Deep | High emotion, relationship at risk |
| Pitch/Proposal | 3+ hours | Deep | Personal investment, acceptance needed |
| One-on-One | 30 min | Medium | Trust and relationship building |
| High-Stakes | As much as needed | Very Deep | Life-changing outcome |

## The Mastery Insight

**The meeting type doesn't matter. What matters is:**

1. Understanding the emotional terrain (what people are protecting)
2. Knowing your goal (not ego goals, actual goals)
3. Preparing so well you're not surprised (removes panic)
4. Maintaining emotional distance when triggered (keeps you effective)
5. Treating the other person's triggers with respect (builds trust)

Musashi didn't fight differently because the opponent was skilled or weak. He fought the terrain. He studied what made his opponent move. He removed surprise through preparation.

You do the same. Know the terrain. Prepare. Stay calm. Win not through force, but through clarity.
